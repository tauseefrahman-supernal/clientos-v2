## 4. Why they bought

Several causes, and they shifted as the buyer inside Labrynth changed.

1. **A sister-company handshake.** The deal started as an agreement between Stuart and Scott inside Infinity, roughly $100K a year, before any scope existed (GTM + Scoping, 30 Jun and 10 Jul 2026; Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 3).
2. **Stuart's real constraint was delivery capacity.** Labrynth delivers through forward-deployed engineers and product people who were saturated, with hiring tied to a one-dollar-in, one-dollar-out rule and a product launch (Project LAX) about to consume everything (Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 5). An agent that frees capacity fitted that rule.
3. **Chris's pain was concrete.** He ran about 10 parallel projects with no room for expectation management, and needed client progress visibility and scope control (GTM + Scoping, 2 Jul 2026). Later work found QA and demo prep take 80 to 90% of his time against a normal 10 to 25% (Notion: Labrynth (Oct 12 - Oct 16)).
4. **Labrynth's tools were already connected.** ClickUp, Granola, Notion and HubSpot were in use, so a system could sit on top rather than replace (Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 3, 7).
5. **Growth without hiring.** The aim was to take Chris from about 8 to 12 to 15 accounts without adding staff (Squad - Metrics Definition, 17 Sep 2026; All-Hands, 10 Nov 2026).
6. **Speed.** Stuart judged anything framed in months as dead; the order form promised a working prototype in month 1 (Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 1; Drive: OF-002 › 2).
7. **What lost.** The content factory lost because Stuart saw it as noise and too slow at about $100K over seven months; he would buy it prebuilt at about $15K a month (Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 8). Why the account management scope won over Stuart's own two priorities (agentic SDLC and RTI automation) is not recorded; one note says the client chose it (Tauseef / Jaime, 14 Aug 2026).

Alternatives on the table: Claude and Claude-based tools already licensed org-wide (Chris kept asking how Cabana Boy differs from Claude and Claude Tag), Grokbot-style configurable agents, and Harold's own 200-plus personal agents (Weekly Review, 23 Oct 2026; Squad Portfolio Sync, 15 Sep 2026; Notion: Labrynth Strategy › 4). Supernal's answer was ownership and data security (Weekly Review, 23 Oct 2026).

### Feature rationale

1. **A Hermes agent in Slack, not a standalone app.** Reason: Hermes brings memory, skills and connections out of the box, and the client reacted better to it than to the earlier app. Decided by 3 Sep 2026 (Squad - Teamback, 3 Sep 2026).
2. **Agent skills and Chris's voice before vault optimisation; no vector database yet.** Reason: skills can improve over time, Chris could eventually run the agent himself, and the team keeps control of what enters the vault for privacy. Decided 11 Sep 2026 (Weekly Review + Next Cycle Plan, 11 Sep 2026).
3. **Restricted access, not redaction.** Reason: chosen in the security review with Athena; agents get full access inside controlled hosting, and any human maintenance access is logged. Decided by 24 Aug 2026 (Delivery Sync, 24 Aug 2026).
4. **A Smart Connector with every client switched off by default.** Reason: lets Granola, Drive and ClickUp connect while keeping Supernal out of Labrynth's data, and avoids the slower fallback of deploying inside Labrynth's infrastructure. Proposed 28 Sep, approved 15 Oct 2026 (Delivery Sync, 28 Sep 2026; Notion: Labrynth (Oct 12 - Oct 16)).
5. **Build the core workflow first (Kickoff Kit plus Daily Delivery Check).** Reason: it carries most of the modeled business case ($376K to $444K a year of $605K to $839K). Decided Oct 2026 (Drive: Weekly Brief W14).
6. **Never guess: a checker blocks any date, name or owner without a source, and gaps become questions to Chris.** Reason: trust in what goes to a client; Chris sees workflows as a convenience over prompting the agent, so output has to be clearly better. Built early Nov 2026 (Drive: Weekly Brief W14; Slack #supernal-squad-the-origin, 5 Nov 2026; Notion: Labrynth (Oct 26 - Oct 30)).
7. **Belongil, not Validity, as the first build client.** Reason: judged a better starting point for workflow design. Decided week of 26 Oct 2026 (Notion: Labrynth (Oct 26 - Oct 30)).
8. **A synthetic SOW to keep building.** Reason: real SOWs were not shared; synthetic data keeps the build moving but cannot validate it. Decided early Nov 2026 (Notion: Labrynth (Nov 2 - Nov 6)).
9. **Adversarial testing: Claude writes hypothetical SOWs, another model critiques the agent's output.** Reason: prove the agent is faithful before demos and give Chris a QA story. Planned 23 Oct 2026 (Weekly Review + Next Cycle Plan, 23 Oct 2026).
10. **Start on one pilot account, then scale to the rest.** Reason: speed to visible value; a counter-view held that one account is too small a sample. Debated 6 Aug 2026 (Strat ², 6 Aug 2026).

## 13. Book

### 1. The account in one page

Labrynth is a small RegTech company, about 18 people and about $3M in annual revenue, that helps governments and the firms that deal with them get through regulation: it checks permit and licensing applications, builds narrow regulatory assistants and publishes a city-level index of regulatory burden. It is also Supernal's sister company. Both are owned by Infinity Constellation, and the order form names Infinity on both sides of the deal.

Supernal is building Labrynth an Account Management OS: in practice an AI account manager called Cabana Boy that lives in Labrynth's Slack and works for one person, Chris Kuss, the Head of Customer Success. The contract, signed on 29 Jul 2026, is $15,000 a month for 12 months, $180,000 in all, with an exit after month 6. An earlier one-month engagement sits in Account OS as $30,000.

Three things set the account apart. The money is internal to Infinity, so the squad counts it as break-even while it still makes up about 40% of the squad's managed revenue. The scope changed twice before signing, from a content factory to Stuart's priorities to customer success. And progress has been gated less by building than by data access and security, because Labrynth handles sensitive government and nuclear work.

Today the security work is done, the connector is live, and the first workflow, which turns a signed SOW into a kickoff deck, plan and task list, works in testing. The core workflow is due in the first week of December. But Labrynth laid off much of its staff in early November, including its executive team, and has since been folded back into Infinity. The account is at risk.

Sources: (Drive: OF-002); (Notion: Labrynth Strategy); (Account OS); (Squad Lab, 6 and 13 Oct 2026); (Delivery Sync, 9 Nov 2026); (Sync: Seb & Tauseef, 13 Nov 2026).

### 2. Why they bought, and why the scope moved twice

The deal began as a handshake between two chief executives inside Infinity: about $100,000 a year, agreed before anyone knew what would be built. Stuart Lacey, Labrynth's CEO, delegated discovery to Harold, his head of product, who steered Supernal toward marketing. By late June the team had a content factory scoped: a knowledge vault, an app and an agent system to produce LinkedIn, X and Substack posts, priced at $7,000 a month.

It did not survive Stuart. When the team finally met him directly on 21 Jul, he said marketing was not his problem. His constraint was delivery capacity: his product and engineering people were saturated, hiring was frozen unless each hire paid for itself, and a new product launch was about to take everyone. He would buy a content factory ready-made, but not wait seven months for one. He asked instead for two things: agents that extend his forward-deployed product and engineering work, and automation of the regulatory index.

What was signed eight days later was neither. It was a system for customer success. The reasons are only partly recorded. Chris had the clearest pain of anyone: about ten parallel projects, no time to keep clients informed or to stop scope creep, and his tools already connected. The account scope fitted Stuart's rule of growing without hiring, since the target was to let Chris take on 12 to 15 accounts instead of 8. One internal note says the client chose this scope. Another says the strategist thought the business case weak. Both are true, and that tension shaped August.

### 3. How the deal is built

The signed order form, OF-002, is a monthly fee for strategy and build, $15,000 a month, $180,000 over the term. It describes four monthly milestones: a data foundation and prototype in month 1, an operational pilot with automated client updates in month 2, extension across a cohort of Labrynth's customers in month 3, and a portfolio command center with a calibrated expectation scorecard in month 4. After that, the fee continues for maintenance and refinement. There are no numeric success targets other than an expectation score of 3 or better.

The parties line is unusual: Infinity Constellation trading as Supernal, selling to Infinity Constellation trading as Labrynth, billed to Infinity's finance team. That is why the squad calls it break-even and internal, and why Account OS showed no revenue for it. Stuart had said he was open to cash, trade or equity, and that the money was not his worry.

The order form puts a lot on the client. Sameen Kashfi approves, Chris runs day to day, and Labrynth must supply a pilot account, the contract repository, ClickUp, Granola and Notion within five business days of a request, keep task statuses accurate, and attend a weekly working session. Delays move dates day for day. Labrynth owns the strategy, code and workflows; Supernal runs the infrastructure.

Delivery has not followed the month-by-month plan. The client deck re-cut the work into strategy, a pilot, a security phase and a workflow build, because security was not in the order form but became the gate for everything.

### 4. The product and why it is built this way

The first prototype in August was a conventional app. By early September it had become something else: a Hermes agent connected to Slack, where Chris already works, with the app kept only as a panel to configure and watch the agent. Hermes was chosen because memory, skills and connections came ready-made, and Chris liked it more than the app. The squad chose to invest in the agent's skills and in Chris's way of writing before tuning the knowledge store, so that the agent could improve over time and Chris could one day own it.

Security drove the next two months. Labrynth works with government and nuclear clients and was cautious about letting an outside system read its meetings and contracts. An early incident, an agent messaging Chris unprompted in a shared channel, did not help. The team chose restricted access over redaction, then designed a Smart Connector: a gateway that holds the credentials, logs every action and keeps each Labrynth client switched off until Labrynth turns it on. Twelve security items were closed between 15 and 30 Oct, and the connector went live on ClickUp and Granola on 30 Oct.

The work now centres on one core workflow. The Kickoff Kit reads a signed SOW and drafts a kickoff deck, an 8-week plan and a ClickUp list, asking questions instead of guessing; a checker refuses any date, name or owner without a source. The Daily Delivery Check will then review each account every day. This pair was put first because it carries most of the modeled value, and because Chris needs to see it beat simply prompting the agent.

### 7. What the account means for the squad
